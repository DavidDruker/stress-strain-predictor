What this can and cannot tell you

  • It cannot see heat treatment. Two bars of identical chemistry, one quenched and tempered at 200 °C and one at 650 °C, differ by 500 MPa or more in yield strength — and reach this model as the same input. Read the numbers as what steels of roughly this chemistry usually do, not as what your heat will do.
  • It is at its measured ceiling, not merely approximate. Bars sharing an exact chemistry in the training data still scatter by 107–191 MPa in yield strength. That scatter is processing history, so it is a floor no chemistry-only model can cross. The model already sits inside it.
  • Unfamiliar alloy families are the real failure mode. On four of twelve held-out families the model did worse than simply guessing the training median. The range check beside the inputs catches out-of-range elements, but a chemistry can sit inside every range and still belong to a family the model has never seen.
  • The stretch figure assumes a proportional specimen. Elongation is only comparable within a gauge-length standard — A5 and A50 mm give different numbers for the same steel — and the training data does not record which standard each row used. Millimetres of stretch inherit that uncertainty; forces do not.
  • The curve between the points is drawn, not predicted. The model outputs three landmarks. No open dataset found reports strain at maximum load or a hardening exponent, so the path between them is a Hollomon power law closed with the Considère condition and pinned to the predicted points. The shape is a reconstruction; only the marked points are predictions.
  • Modelled as a solid round bar. A stranded wire rope of the same nominal area carries roughly 10–15% less, because the helical lay means the wires do not all pull along the axis. Treat these forces as an upper bound for rope.
  • Not for design allowables. These are statistical predictions from 1,359 heats, 55% of which are specification minima rather than measurements. They are not a substitute for a tensile test.
